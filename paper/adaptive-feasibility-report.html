Can AdaptiShield be made adaptive, or is it stuck with hardcoded rules?

I ran the one experiment that could answer this on real data overnight. Here is the honest result, and exactly what each path to a genuinely adaptive system would cost.

Bottom line

The adaptive machinery is real and works — but the signal it needs to learn from is mostly not there, and last night's test explains why in a way that is now measured, not guessed.

The system can be made adaptive. But the cheap route I hoped for — the one that would have fixed everything at once — does not work on the current model, and I can now show that with numbers rather than argue it. The reliable route to adaptivity is a different, smaller change that sidesteps the problem entirely.

None of this is doable before 14 September, and none of it needs to be. It is the next paper, not this one.

Your two questions, answered directly

Everything below is the evidence for these

1. Can we make it adaptive, not just hardcoded rules?

Yes — but "adaptive" splits into two very different projects. The learning loop you already built works (it closes a gap and generalises). What it lacks is something to learn from. There are two ways to give it that: a hard research route (fix the signal itself) that last night showed is uncertain, and a practical route (learn the safe-recipient list) that is reliable but modest. Details below.

2. What changes do I need to make?

For the September paper: none. The research is complete. For a genuinely adaptive follow-up: either replace the 0/1/2 scorer with a continuous one and move to a larger model (weeks–months, uncertain), or make the egress allow-list learn per deployment (about two weeks, reliable). The evidence says start with the second.

How we got here — adaptive by design, hardcoded by measurement

Nobody chose this; each phase measured a bit more of why

The project never abandoned adaptivity as a decision. What happened is that phase after phase measured that the adaptive layer had nothing to act on:

  • The loop works. Given a gap its dial can close, it observes the miss, proposes a fix that carries no memorised attacker address, and the fix generalises to an attacker it never saw. That is a real, tested, adaptive mechanism.
  • But no such gap arises naturally. Replaying every possible dial setting across 118 attacks catches zero extra. The one gain it ever found was an artefact of our own small test set — 36 false alarms out of 68 on outside data.
  • Phase 15 found the root cause. The dial reads a "contrast" — how much the assistant's judgement shifts when suspicious text is hidden. On 24 of 30 realistic turns that contrast was zero. A dial reading a flat signal has nothing to turn.

So the real sentence is not "we ended up with hardcoded rules." It is: the adaptive mechanism works and has nothing to learn from, because the quantity it reads is flat. Last night's job was to find out why it is flat — and whether that is fixable.

The experiment I ran overnight

Read-only · touched no shipped code · no re-measurement triggered

The detector scores each action on a blunt 3-point scale: 0, 1, or 2. The suspicion was that this rounding is what flattens the contrast — two genuinely different behaviours both round to the same integer, so the difference reads as zero even when the model's underlying confidence differs.

To test that, I bypassed the 3-point score and read the model's raw confidence directly. For all 15 Phase 15 turns, I asked the model a forced yes/no — "would you carry out this instruction?" — under three conditions (with the user's real task, with only the suspicious text, and with the suspicious text cleaned) and measured the exact probability it placed on each answer. That gives a smooth, continuous number where the detector only had three steps.

What a good result would have looked like: the continuous contrast is large on attacks and small on safe documents — proving the rounding threw away real signal, and that a smooth scorer makes the whole architecture adaptive again.

What the numbers actually said

results/phase15/logprob_probe.json · gemma3:4b · one line per turn

Three things came out, and they don't all point the same way.

Finding 1 — Yes, the rounding does destroy signal

On all 5 turns where the 3-point score gave a flat contrast, the continuous measure was not flat — every one moved by more than 0.5, and several by 10–30. So the suspicion was correct: the blunt scorer is throwing information away. That vindicates the diagnosis.

Finding 2 — But the recovered signal doesn't tell attacks from safe documents

The main contrast (call it "does the user's task change the model's mind") is just as large on safe documents as on attacks — average 7.1 vs 7.5, and the single biggest value of all belongs to a benign onboarding message. It measures "is this relevant to the task", not "is this an attack." So recovering the signal is not enough; the recovered signal is the wrong signal.

Every turn. "Cleaning contrast" is the one measure that separated the classes — non-zero only on attacks — but it fires on just 3 of 9, and sits on a saturated base (see Finding 3).

There is one hopeful thread. The "cleaning contrast" — how much the model's confidence drops when the suspicious text is cleaned up — was exactly zero on all 6 safe turns and non-zero on 3 of the 9 attacks. That is real directional separation, and it is exactly the signal the detector is supposed to use. The problem is it fired on only a third of attacks, and the amounts are tiny.

Finding 3 — The model is too sure of itself for this to work cleanly

35 of 45 measurements were "saturated" — the model was so certain of its yes/no (by margins of 20+) that the numbers stop being trustworthy. The 3 promising attack signals above (0.85 to 3.2) are tiny ripples sitting on top of these huge saturated values. On a small 4-billion-parameter model doing a forced yes/no, the confidence pins to the ceiling and the useful gradient disappears. That is the real obstacle, and it is a property of the model, not the idea.

So: can it be made adaptive? Four honest routes

Updated by last night's evidence

1 · Continuous causal contrast

Uncertain — needs a bigger model

Replace the 3-point scorer with the smooth confidence measure. Last night showed this recovers real signal but the main contrast is the wrong one and the useful one saturates on a 4B model. To work it needs a larger, better-calibrated model where the confidence doesn't pin to the ceiling — and even then, only the "cleaning contrast" thread looks alive. This is the route that would make the existing architecture adaptive, and it is now a genuine research programme with an uncertain outcome, not a tweak.

Effort monthsRisk high — may still be flatKeeps the causal idea yes

2 · Learn the safe-recipient list

Reliable — the one to build

The single part of the detector that works reliably is: does the action name an address or link lifted from the untrusted text? What sits around it — the list of allowed destinations — is currently fixed. Make that learn per deployment: which recipients and hosts are normal for this user, treat a novel one as suspicious. Real deployments are narrow (a finance team emails the same 40 people), so there is genuine signal here. It also turns today's known false positive — flagging a real colleague's address — into the adaptive feature instead of a limitation.

Effort ~2 weeksRisk lowKeeps the causal idea alongside it

3 · Learn the harm vocabulary

Possible — no test set left

Phase 13 hand-wrote a list of what counts as harmful and got 43% on fresh attacks. A trained classifier could likely beat that. Two costs: it drifts toward "we built a detector", losing the causal framing that makes the work distinctive; and both outside attack sets are already used up as measuring sticks, so an honest test needs a third one that doesn't exist yet.

Effort ~3 weeks + new dataRisk mediumKeeps the causal idea weakens it

4 · Behavioural baseline per agent

Works — but a different paper

Learn what this assistant normally does — which tools, which destinations — and flag deviation. This sidesteps the flat-signal problem completely because it never touches the causal contrast. It is genuinely adaptive and would work. It is also a different research direction and abandons the causal idea rather than repairing it.

Effort ~4 weeksRisk lowKeeps the causal idea no

What I recommend

In order

  • Submit in September as-is. The paper is a strong, honest, complete negative result. Nothing here changes that, and none of these routes is finishable in time.
  • Rename the paper (not the system). Keep "AdaptiShield" — the system genuinely has an adaptive layer. But don't let the title claim adaptivity as a proven result, because the paper reports it as a measured no-op. Reviewers punish over-claiming far harder than an honest negative.
  • Make last night's test part of the paper. It upgrades the limitations section from "a smoother scorer might help" to "we measured it: the rounding does destroy signal, but a naive smooth replacement saturates on a small model — here is the one thread that survived." That is a real contribution and it sets up the follow-up.
  • For the next paper, build Route 2 first. It is the reliable adaptive win, it is about two weeks, and it makes the one component that actually works into the adaptive one. Route 1 is the ambitious follow-up after that, and it needs a bigger model before it is worth the compute.
The one-sentence answer. It can be adaptive — but the honest, reliable way is to make the part that works (the recipient check) learn, not to keep trying to squeeze a learning signal out of the part that doesn't (the causal contrast), which last night showed is flat on this model for a reason that is now measured rather than assumed.

You did not build a defence that failed. You built one, measured exactly where it stops working, and last night measured why the adaptive part had nothing to learn from. That "why" is the seed of the next paper.
